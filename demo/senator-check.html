Where the information comes from

SenatorsActual Senate votes, collected and scored by Voteview, a university project. Every vote a senator has cast this Congress is boiled down to one overall voting pattern, from more liberal to more conservative.
State votersAcademic survey estimates from the American Ideology Project (Tausanovitch & Warshaw), 2020 wave: where voters in each state generally sit, from surveys run 2017–2021.
PopulationU.S. Census estimates, used to weight states when we describe the country as a whole.
Bills and committeesOfficial congressional records: each bill's sponsor, the committees it went to, and whether a committee formally sent it forward.

How we compare them

Two lines, left to right On these charts, left means more liberal and right means more conservative. Each line runs from 0 (most liberal) to 100 (most conservative). The senator scores and the voter estimates are each published on a −1 to +1 scale; we show both as 0 to 100 because it is easier to read, but they remain two different scales.
Why the two are shown separately A senator's voting pattern is compared with the Senate middle, on the senators' scale. A state's voter estimate is compared with the national estimate, on the voters' scale. A direct senator-versus-state comparison would need a validated statistical bridge between the two scales, and CivicAlign does not currently have one, so it does not calculate whether a senator is left or right of their state.
What it cannot tell you Which issues make up a gap, or how a state's voters feel about any one bill. Each number squeezes many positions into one, so this page says how far apart overall, not why, and this page does not guess.

Important limits

  • The senator and voter measures are built differently; they come from different rulers and are not compared with each other.
  • A direct senator-versus-state alignment measure needs a validated bridge between the two scales. CivicAlign does not currently have that bridge.
  • The voter estimate currently uses the 2020 wave of the American Ideology Project.
  • A state's overall position does not tell us how its voters feel about one specific bill.
  • Where senators divided on a vote does not measure whether the bill itself was liberal or conservative.
  • A bill's sponsor having a liberal or conservative record does not make the bill liberal or conservative.
  • Bills still in committee are not automatically dead.
  • Small samples deserve weak conclusions.

Every figure on this site is recomputed from the source files by an independent check before each weekly update is published.
