How far is your senator from your state?

Your senators and your state's voters are both placed on the same scale, from most liberal to most conservative. This measures the exact distance between them.

How this works

  1. Place your senators on a scale Every roll-call vote a senator has cast, turned into one number from −1 (most liberal) to +1 (most conservative).
  2. Place your state's voters on the same scale Large surveys of residents in every state, translated by academic researchers into the exact same numbers, so the two can be compared directly.
  3. Measure the distance between them Subtract one from the other. The further apart, the less your senator reflects the people who elected them. That distance is the score.

What this can and cannot tell you

Your state's position is an estimate, not a headcount.
It comes from large academic surveys modelled down to the state level. It carries a margin of error of roughly 0.04, which is small next to the gaps shown here but is not zero.
It compares whole records, not individual issues.
A senator's number summarises every vote they have cast into one figure. Someone can match their state overall while voting against it on the issue you care most about.
Almost every senator is more extreme than their state.
The typical senator scores —. A gap is normal in today's Senate, so the useful question is not whether there is one but whether it is bigger than most.
It says nothing about any single vote.
This is a senator's whole record at once. It cannot tell you how they voted on one bill you care about.
