Plain answers to the questions people ask most. We combine public voting records, survey estimates, and other public data to make these comparisons.

What is this site showing me?

How each senator’s overall Senate voting record compares with senators in the same caucus group representing states that voted similarly in recent presidential elections, with the actual votes behind that record. It also shows how equal representation of states shapes the Senate, and whether a committee looks or behaves differently from the Senate overall.

How are senators measured?

From their actual Senate votes, collected and scored by Voteview, a university project. Every vote a senator has cast this Congress is boiled down to one overall voting pattern, from more liberal to more conservative.

Who are the “comparable senators”?

Senators in the same caucus group who represent other states whose average two-party presidential vote across the last three elections was within 4 points of your state’s. Your state’s own senators are never each other’s peers. CivicAlign shows a conclusion only with at least six such peers, and only if it is the same whether the window is 2, 3, 4 or 5 points; otherwise it says so. This compares senators with senators. It does not measure whether a senator agrees with the state’s voters.

What is a “caucus group”?

The Republican caucus, or the Democratic caucus: Democrats plus the Independents who caucus with them, as recorded in the current Senate roster. An Independent is shown as an Independent; they are grouped with the caucus they belong to only for this comparison. A senator without a verified caucus group gets no peer comparison.

Where do the election results come from?

The MIT Election Data and Science Lab’s official presidential returns. CivicAlign uses each state’s two-party share (Republican and Democratic candidates only), averaged equally over the last three elections.

What is the voter estimate?

An academic survey estimate from the American Ideology Project (Tausanovitch & Warshaw), 2020 wave: where voters in each state generally sit politically, from surveys run 2017–2021. It is not a current opinion poll, an election result, party registration or an approval rating.

Are the senator and voter measures directly compared?

No. They use different measurement systems, so CivicAlign shows them separately. A direct senator-versus-state comparison would need a validated statistical bridge between the two scales, and CivicAlign does not currently have one.

What does Senate middle mean?

The center of current senators’ voting patterns. Each senator, each committee and the 60-vote point are shown compared with it.

Why compare Senate seats with the national vote?

Every state has two seats, so the Senate represents states equally, not people equally. Averaging each seat’s state presidential vote and comparing it with the national vote shows how much that changes the political mix of the Senate. Both figures are election results, so they are on the same scale. It is a fact about seats, not about any senator.

Why are 60 votes shown?

Under Senate rules, ending debate on most legislation generally requires three-fifths of senators: 60 votes when all 100 seats are filled. So the senator around the 60-vote point can matter as much as the middle. Not every bill needs 60 votes.

What does a Senate committee do?

It reviews bills in its subject area before many of them can go to the full Senate. Bills are introduced, sent to a committee, and some are formally sent on to the full Senate, which may then vote.

What does “sent forward” mean?

Formally reported by the committee to the full Senate. Not every reported bill gets a floor vote, and a bill still in committee is not dead: Congress is still in session and some bills may move later.

Does this tell me whether my senator represents me?

No. CivicAlign shows voting patterns and voter estimates. Whether those differences matter to you depends on the issues you care about.

Does this tell me whether a bill is liberal or conservative?

No. The floor-vote measure shows where senators divided on a vote, not whether the bill itself was liberal or conservative. A sponsor’s voting record does not tell us that either.

What can’t this tell me?

Which issues make up a difference, or how a state’s voters feel about any one bill: each number squeezes many positions into one. Small samples deserve weak conclusions. Words like “near” and “on the more liberal side” describe position by one fixed rule; they are not grades.

Every figure on this site is recomputed from the source files by an independent check before each weekly update is published.

Show the technical methodology
Two lines, left to right On these charts, left means more liberal and right means more conservative. Each line runs from 0 (the liberal end) to 100 (the conservative end). The senator scores and the voter estimates are each published on a −1 to +1 scale; we show both as 0 to 100 because it is easier to read, but they remain two different scales.
Why the two are shown separately A senator's voting pattern is compared with the Senate middle, on the senators' scale. A state's voter estimate is compared with the national estimate, on the voters' scale. The senator and voter measures are built differently and are not compared with each other, so this page does not calculate whether a senator is left or right of their state.

CivicAlign converts Voteview's academic voting-position scale, and the survey's voter scale, into easier-to-display lines from 0 to 100. A number is a position on that line; it is not a percentage, a vote total, an approval rating or a grade. Differences on one line are given in display-scale units.
