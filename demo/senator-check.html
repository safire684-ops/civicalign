How far is your senator from your state?

Your senators and your state's voters are both placed on the same scale, from most liberal to most conservative. This measures the exact distance between them.

How this works

  1. Place your senators on a scale Every roll-call vote a senator has cast, turned into one number from −1 (most liberal) to +1 (most conservative).
  2. Place your state's voters on the same scale Large surveys of residents in every state, translated by academic researchers into the exact same numbers, so the two can be compared directly.
  3. Measure the distance between them Subtract one from the other. The further apart, the less your senator reflects the people who elected them. That distance is the score.

Why that matters

Because the Senate sits to the right of the national centre, a bill backed by a slim majority of the American public can still fail in the Senate, while a bill favoured mainly by smaller conservative-leaning states can pass without majority support nationally. The skew does not decide any single vote, but it shifts which bills are able to pass at all.

The committee gatekeepers

Before a bill can reach the Senate floor, it must first pass through a committee. If a committee sits well to one side of the Senate as a whole, bills that the full chamber might pass can die there instead.

What this can and cannot tell you

Your state's position is an estimate, not a headcount.
It comes from large academic surveys modelled down to the state level. It carries a margin of error of roughly 0.04, which is small next to the gaps shown here but is not zero.
It compares whole records, not individual issues.
A senator's number summarises every vote they have cast into one figure. Someone can match their state overall while voting against it on the issue you care most about.
The match percentage is not a share of votes.
A 70% representation match does not mean the senator voted your way 70 times out of 100. It is a distance on the ideological scale, converted to a percentage so it can be compared across senators.
Almost every senator is more extreme than their state.
The typical senator scores —. A gap is normal in today's Senate, so the useful question is not whether there is one but whether it is bigger than most.
It says nothing about any single vote.
This is a senator's whole record at once. It cannot tell you how they voted on one bill you care about.

Check our work
How we scored the senator We took every roll-call vote they have cast this session and compared their pattern of yes and no votes against every other member of Congress. That places them at one exact point on the liberal–conservative scale.
How we scored the state We used large academic surveys of residents in every state, asking about their actual policy preferences. Researchers translate those answers onto the very same scale used for senators, so the two can be compared directly rather than guessed at.
How we compare them We subtract one position from the other. That distance is the whole measurement — there is no weighting, adjustment or judgement applied on top of it.
