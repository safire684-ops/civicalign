Methodology · 119th Congress

How we measure Senate representation

We compare each senator's voting record with the pattern typically seen from senators representing states that vote like theirs, measure how equal representation of states shapes the Senate, and read each committee against the Senate overall. This explains where each number comes from and what it can and cannot tell you.

As of {{asof}}

The question

How does the voting record of the people representing you compare with the political context of the state they represent?

The useful form of that question is relative: given how a state has voted in recent presidential elections, is its senator's overall Senate voting record about what senators from states like it typically show, or does it differ from that pattern? Answering even that properly turns out to be harder than it looks, and most of the work goes into one specific obstacle.

The hard part

A senator is measured by how they vote on bills. Voters are measured by what they tell a survey. Those are two different rulers with two different zero points, so subtracting one from the other is like subtracting Celsius from Fahrenheit: you get a number, and it means nothing.

We use the best available data for each side: Voteview's scores for senators, and academic researchers' survey-based estimates for each state's residents. Both run from liberal to conservative and both fall between −1 and +1. But they were built separately, and nobody has tied the two rulers together exactly. The survey scale is set to its own average and spread; the senator scale comes from votes. So the tool does not put the two on one line, subtract them, or say which side of the state a senator falls on. A direct senator-versus-state alignment measure on those two scales requires a validated statistical bridge between voter and legislator scales, and CivicAlign does not have one.

The way round it is to use a public measure that needs no survey and no bridge: how the state actually voted. Across all 100 current senators, a state's recent presidential vote and its senator's voting position line up strongly. That relationship gives an expected voting position for a state with any given result, on the senators' own scale, so the senator and the expectation can be compared directly (section 1). The survey estimate of a state's voters is still shown, on its own scale, as separate context (section 1b).

Each scale you see runs from 0 to 100. 0 is most liberal, 100 most conservative, 50 the middle. Under the hood both sets of numbers run from −1 to +1; we show each rescaled (position × 50 + 50, so a difference of 0.10 becomes 5 points) because 0 to 100 is easier to read. They remain two different scales. The raw numbers are in the tool under "Show the math".

Two details that sound small and are not. We use the per-Congress version of the senator score, because the better-known version is a single number fixed for a senator's whole career and could never show anyone changing. And the government's own file lists 104 senators for 100 seats, because members who left mid-term stay in it beside their replacements, so we join against the current roster to get the real 100.

The three measurements

1. Your senator, against the pattern for states that vote like yours

This is the tool's primary result for a senator, and it never subtracts a survey figure from a voting score. Instead it uses election results, which are public behaviour measured directly. For each of the {{r_n}} current senators with a score, take two numbers: the senator's Voteview voting position, and their state's share of the two-party presidential vote averaged equally over {{r_years}} (MIT Election Data and Science Lab). Fit one straight line through all {{r_n}} points: position = {{r_intercept}} + {{r_slope}} × state share. The line says what voting position a state's recent presidential vote typically goes with; the state pattern accounts for {{r_r2}} of the variation in senators' records (r²), and the slope's 95% interval, {{r_slope_lo}} to {{r_slope_hi}}, is far from zero.

Expected position and typical range. Reading the line at a state's share gives the expected position for a state that votes like it. The typical range around it is one standard error of prediction (residual standard error {{r_resid_se}}, about {{r_resid_se_100}} display-scale units, widened slightly at the ends of the vote-share range); roughly two thirds of senators sit inside it. Both senators from a state are compared with the same expected position, because the input is the state's vote, not the senator's party.

The words follow one rule, driven by the model's own uncertainty, not by hand-picked buckets. On the tool the expected position is called the "typical position based on the state's recent presidential voting". A senator inside the typical range is "within the typical range" ({{r_within}} of {{r_n}} today). Outside it, the record is "more liberal" or "more conservative than the typical range" ({{r_beyond}} today). When the residual divided by its own standard error, corrected for leverage, also exceeds 2, the tool says the record is "well outside the typical range" ({{r_clear}} today); it makes no claim about chance or significance on the page, because the model omits party and pairs two senators per state, which the standard errors do not account for. No senator is ranked or scored.

What this does and does not answer. It answers: how does this senator's overall record compare with what senators from states with a similar recent presidential vote typically look like? It does not answer how far a senator sits from their state's voters in absolute terms; that still needs a validated bridge between the voter and legislator scales, which does not exist. Three limits are worth knowing. The pattern is relative: if every senator moved together, no residual would change. The line ignores party, so in a closely divided state the expected position sits between the two parties and senators of both parties tend to fall on their own side of it; averaged across the Senate, Democrats' records sit {{r_dem_resid}} and Republicans' {{r_rep_resid}} display-scale units from the line. And two senators share each state's input, so the 100 observations are not fully independent; the standard errors treat them as if they were, which slightly overstates certainty. None of this is a judgement about any senator.

The actual votes. Each senator card lists their recorded Yea or Nay on the most recent Senate votes on passing a bill, the same votes for every senator, newest first, with a link to the roll call at Voteview. They show what the record is made of. They carry no description of what a bill did (that needs a verified summary source, which is a separate piece of this project) and no claim about what the state's voters wanted.

Words for positions: one fixed rule

The tool describes every same-scale position in words before any number, and the words follow one rule. Take the difference between a position and its reference point on the underlying scale: within {{rel_near_raw}} ({{rel_near}} display-scale units) the position is "near" the reference; beyond that it is "on the more liberal side" or "on the more conservative side" of the reference. The words give the direction; the chart shows how far, and the exact distance is under "See details". The same rule is used for a senator against the Senate middle, a state's estimate against the national voter estimate, a committee's members against the Senate middle, and the 60-vote point. They are neutral descriptions of relative position, not grades; no senator is ranked.

Units. Both scales are shown on a 0-to-100 display line. A number is a position on that line; it is not a percentage, a vote total, an approval rating or a grade. Differences along one line are given in "display-scale units" (underlying difference × 50). Bill-flow rates are genuine percentages and their differences are "percentage points"; the two are never mixed.

2. How Senate seats represent the country's vote

Every state elects two senators whether it holds 40 million people or 600,000, so a Senate seat represents states equally, not people equally. How much that changes the Senate's political mix can be measured with election results on both sides, so no ideology scale is involved: average each seat's state share of the two-party presidential vote ({{r_years}}, each election weighted equally) and compare it with the national share. Across the 100 seats the average state voted {{seats_gop}}% Republican; the country voted {{nat_gop}}%. The mix of states represented by Senate seats is therefore {{sk_points}} percentage points more {{sk_side}} than the national vote. Taken one election at a time the figure runs from {{sk_lo}} to {{sk_hi}} points, so it does not depend on which cycle is chosen. This is a fact about which states hold seats, not about any senator's opinions, and the tool describes it without calling it fair or unfair.

Every state elects two senators whether it holds 40 million people or 600,000, so the Senate does not represent people in proportion to where they live. That does not decide any single vote, but it shapes which bills can pass at all.

3. Committees: where the members sit, and what they passed

Each committee is measured against the Senate's middle ({{ch_m}}). Both sides of that comparison come from Voteview, so it is stated in points.

Where the members sit. The midpoint of the committee's members on the same scale. A midpoint more than 7.5 display-scale units (0.15 underlying) from the Senate's middle is flagged, under "More about this committee", as sitting well to one side: the position of a gatekeeper. This number is soft: on a committee split between two parties the midpoint can fall in the gap between them, and it can move sharply when one member is replaced. The last column says how far.

{{cnd_table}}

Where the Senate split on its bills. Take the bills a committee sent to the floor, and the dividing line of every floor vote on them: the point on the scale that best separates the senators who voted Yes from those who voted No. Their median is where the Senate split on that committee's bills. This says where the Yes and No sides divided, not whether the bills themselves were liberal or conservative: a party-line vote on any bill splits the Senate near the middle. Only {{oi_count}} committees have seven or more such votes; most floor action is nominations and House bills that no Senate committee reported.

4. What each committee has sent forward

Almost every bill goes to a committee before the full Senate can vote on it, and most are still sitting there. So instead of asking who sits on a committee, we look at what it has actually done: of the bills sent to it, how many has it sent on to the full Senate so far? A bill that has not been sent on is counted as "not yet", never as dead: this Congress is not over, and some will still move.

Each bill takes the side of the senator who wrote it, using that senator's voting record. A bill by a senator with a liberal record counts as liberal. Then, for every committee, we compare how often each side's bills got through.

The rates are stark on their own. Of {{gk_referred}} distinct bills sent to a committee this Congress, {{gk_reported}} have been sent on so far — about {{gk_pct}}%. (Some bills go to more than one committee, so the committee-by-committee counts add up to {{gk_referrals}} referrals.) The {{gk_worst_name}} Committee has sent on {{gk_worst_rep}} of {{gk_worst_ref}}.

The adjustment that matters. Across the whole Senate, bills by conservative senators get through {{gk_base}} percentage points more often than bills by liberal senators. Whatever its causes, that Senate-wide difference is the pattern every committee shares. If we compared with zero instead, nearly every committee would look the same way and the measure would say nothing about any one of them. So each committee is compared with that {{gk_base}}-point Senate-wide pattern, not with zero.

Every committee's counts are shown on the tool, but the two sides are only compared where each side has at least 25 bills, because below that a single bill moves the percentage by several points. {{gk_n_reportable}} of the committees clear that bar.

What is counted, exactly. Senate bills written by current senators, as recorded in the government's bill-status files downloaded on {{gk_asof}}. "Sent on" means the committee formally reported the bill to the full Senate. A bill approved at a markup meeting but not yet formally reported does not count yet, and House bills sent to Senate committees are not counted, because their authors have no Senate voting record to place them by. So these counts can run below a committee's own tally of bills it has "advanced"; they can be checked bill by bill on Congress.gov.

Why three measures. Members tell you who sits there; the split on its bills tells you how the Senate divided over what it sent on; the rates tell you what it has and has not sent on so far. Each has a weakness the others do not, so the tool shows all three with the count behind each.

What this cannot tell you

It says nothing about any single vote.
A senator's number summarises their whole record into one figure. Someone can match their state overall while voting against it on the issue you care most about.
Politics is squeezed onto one line.
A single liberal-to-conservative axis describes congressional voting well, because most roll-call votes split along it. It describes voters less well, since people's economic and social views do not line up as neatly.
The state comparison is relative, and it is not a poll.
The "typical position based on your state's recent presidential voting" is a pattern across all 100 current senators, read from election results. It says how a senator compares with that pattern, not how far they sit from their own state's voters, and it does not say whether a senator is right or wrong. A departure from the pattern has many possible causes, party first among them.
The voter survey and the voting scores are not compared with each other.
The senator scale is built from votes and the state survey scale from survey answers, each on its own footing, and nobody has tied them together. A direct senator-versus-state alignment measure on those two scales requires a validated statistical bridge between voter and legislator scales. CivicAlign does not currently have that bridge, so it makes no such claim; the state comparison it does make uses election results, on the senators' scale throughout.
The state figures are estimates.
They carry one standard error of about 2 points, which varies a little by state and is drawn on the tool as a dashed band around the state's mark. One standard error is a modest band, covering roughly two thirds of the likely values. It is small next to gaps of 30 or 45 points, but it is not zero. The senator scores have their own error too, which Voteview does not publish, so we cannot carry it through — that is the largest remaining hole in the method.
The survey data lags.
The most recent published wave is 2020, built from surveys run 2017–2021. No newer one exists to use. State opinion shifts, so a fast-changing state is described less well than a stable one.
A bill's side comes from its author, not its content.
We label a bill liberal or conservative by the voting record of the senator who wrote it. That is a reasonable stand-in, but a moderate bill written by a conservative senator counts as conservative. Reading the bills themselves would be more precise, and is part of a separate piece of this project.
A bill can sit in committee for reasons other than politics.
Many bills are duplicates, symbolic, or folded into larger bills rather than passed on their own, and this Congress is not over. The low rates reflect that as much as any gatekeeping. The comparison between the two sides is what carries the finding, not the raw rate.

How to check it

Every figure here can be reproduced with a calculator and a spreadsheet. Download the files in the table above. For the state comparison, take each senator's nokken_poole_dim1 from the Voteview file and their state's two-party presidential share from the MIT file (Republican votes divided by Republican plus Democratic, averaged over the three elections), fit a straight line through the 100 points, and read each senator's distance from it. For the seat comparison, average the 100 seats' state shares and subtract the national share. For a senator's place in the Senate, subtract the median of the 100 scores. For a state's survey estimate, read its mrp_ideology in the ideology file and subtract the population-weighted national figure. Never subtract the ideology file from the Voteview file; the tool does not.

The code that produces these numbers is checked by a test suite that recomputes every published figure straight from the downloaded files, using nothing from the code it is testing — so a bug in the pipeline cannot hide behind a test that shares it. Every download is stamped with its date and a checksum, because Voteview revises scores as new votes are recorded.

If you want to argue with something here, the useful places are the choices rather than the arithmetic: whether one liberal-to-conservative line is a fair summary of politics, whether 2020 survey data is close enough for a 2026 judgement, and whether a bill's author is a fair guide to which side the bill is on. Those are real questions. The arithmetic is not in doubt.
