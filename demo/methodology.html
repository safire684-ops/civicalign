Methodology · 119th Congress

How we measure Senate representation

We compare each senator's voting record with same-party senators representing states that voted similarly in recent presidential elections, measure how equal representation of states shapes the Senate, and read each committee against the Senate overall. This explains where each number comes from and what it can and cannot tell you.

As of 23 September 2026

The question

How does the voting record of the people representing you compare with the political context of the state they represent?

The useful form of that question is relative: given how a state has voted in recent presidential elections, is its senator's overall Senate voting record about what senators from states like it typically show, or does it differ from that pattern? Answering even that properly turns out to be harder than it looks, and most of the work goes into one specific obstacle.

The hard part

A senator is measured by how they vote on bills. Voters are measured by what they tell a survey. Those are two different rulers with two different zero points, so subtracting one from the other is like subtracting Celsius from Fahrenheit: you get a number, and it means nothing.

We use the best available data for each side: Voteview's scores for senators, and academic researchers' survey-based estimates for each state's residents. Both run from liberal to conservative and both fall between −1 and +1. But they were built separately, and nobody has tied the two rulers together exactly. The survey scale is set to its own average and spread; the senator scale comes from votes. So the tool does not put the two on one line, subtract them, or say which side of the state a senator falls on. A direct senator-versus-state alignment measure on those two scales requires a validated statistical bridge between voter and legislator scales, and CivicAlign does not have one.

The way round it is to use a public measure that needs no survey and no bridge: how the state actually voted. That lets the tool pick, for each senator, the comparable senators: same party, other states, similar recent presidential vote. The senator is then compared with those peers on the senators' own scale (section 1). The survey estimate of a state's voters is still shown, on its own scale, as separate context (section 1b).

Each scale you see runs from 0 to 100. 0 is most liberal, 100 most conservative, 50 the middle. Under the hood both sets of numbers run from −1 to +1; we show each rescaled (position × 50 + 50, so a difference of 0.10 becomes 5 points) because 0 to 100 is easier to read. They remain two different scales. The raw numbers are in the tool under "Show the math".

Two details that sound small and are not. We use the per-Congress version of the senator score, because the better-known version is a single number fixed for a senator's whole career and could never show anyone changing. And the government's own file lists 104 senators for 100 seats, because members who left mid-term stay in it beside their replacements, so we join against the current roster to get the real 100.

The three measurements

1. Your senator, against same-party senators from similarly voting states

This is the tool's primary result for a senator. It compares senators with senators, on one scale, and its reference group is something a reader can see.

The rule, fixed. A senator's peers are the current senators who (1) belong to the same party group (Republicans; or Democrats together with the Independents who caucus with them) and (2) represent a different state whose average two-party presidential vote across 2016, 2020, 2024 (MIT Election Data and Science Lab) is within ±4 points of the senator's state. The senator's own state is excluded entirely, so the two senators from one state never serve as each other's peers; two same-party senators from one state are compared with the same pool of other states. A conclusion is published only with at least 6 peers, and only if it is the same at every one of ±2, ±3, ±4, ±5 points that also meets the minimum. Nothing widens the window to find peers.

Why ±4 and 6. A sensitivity run over windows of ±2, ±3, ±4 and ±5 points and minimums of 5, 6 and 8 found that ±2 leaves 17 senators without six peers and ±3 leaves 11, most of them senators from competitive states, which are the ones a reader most wants to see; ±4 leaves three (both Wyoming senators and Maine's Republican) while peer states still span only about five points of vote share; ±5 adds little coverage and widens the group. The cross-window check exists so that the choice of window cannot manufacture a result: nine senators' conclusions change between windows, and the tool says so for each of them rather than picking one.

What is published. For each senator: the observed lowest and highest peer record and the peer middle (median) on the senators' scale, the senator's own record, and one of five sentences: within the observed range; outside it on the more liberal side; outside it on the more conservative side; the comparison changes with the window; too few peers. Today: 75 within, 13 outside, 9 window-dependent (Adam B. Schiff (CA); Richard J. Durbin (IL); Jerry Moran (KS); Tina Smith (MN); Eric Schmitt (MO); Thom Tillis (NC); John Fetterman (PA); John R. Curtis (UT); Mike Lee (UT)), 3 with too few peers (Susan M. Collins (ME); Cynthia M. Lummis (WY); John Barrasso (WY)). No senator is ranked, and the peer senators are listed by state, never ordered by position.

Worked example: Georgia. Georgia's two-party vote ran 2016: 52.7%, 2020: 49.9%, 2024: 51.1%, average 51.2% Republican. Jon Ossoff's peers are the 12 Democratic senators from Arizona, Michigan, Minnesota, New Hampshire, Nevada, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin. Their records run from 27.1 to 41.9 on the display line, middle 35.4; Ossoff's is 22.9. The tool says: "Ossoff’s voting record falls outside that peer range on the more liberal side." Across the windows the conclusion is ±2: outside liberal; ±3: outside liberal; ±4: outside liberal; ±5: outside liberal. For Raphael Warnock, with the same peer pool, it says: "Warnock’s voting record falls outside that peer range on the more liberal side."

What this does and does not answer. It answers: compared with same-party senators representing states with a similar recent presidential vote, where does this senator's overall record sit? It does not answer whether the senator agrees with, matches, or represents the state's voters; nothing about voters enters the comparison. It does not say what any senator should do. And it is only as good as the peer group: where peers are few or the conclusion depends on the window, the tool says so instead of concluding.

The regression that was retired from the page. Until this version the page led with a line fitted through all 100 senators, position on the state's presidential vote, and called the line's value the "typical position" for a state that votes like yours. An audit found that the line mostly measures the party split. Its r² is 0.69 and its slope 4.00, but the residuals split by party (Democrats −7.1, Republicans +6.3 display-scale units on average), and within a party the state's vote explains little: the within-party slope is 1.31 for Democrats (correlation 0.54) and 0.18 for Republicans (correlation 0.06). Worse, for an evenly divided state the line predicts 51.0 with a one-error band of ±14 units, and only 12 of 100 senators sit inside that band: the "typical position" fell in a gap between the two parties where almost nobody sits, so 14 of the 24 senators from competitive states were "outside the typical range" by construction. Adding party to the model changed the classification of 39 senators. A fitted line that behaves this way should not be a public headline about a person; the peer comparison above replaces it. The regression code is kept for diagnostics and for this audit, and is not used for any published classification.

The actual votes. Each senator card lists their recorded Yea or Nay on the most recent Senate votes on passing a bill, the same votes for every senator, newest first, with a link to the roll call at Voteview. They show what the record is made of. They carry no description of what a bill did (that needs a verified summary source, which is a separate piece of this project) and no claim about what the state's voters wanted.

All three were dropped: the two scales are not bridged, so even the sign of that comparison is not a validated finding.

Words for positions: one fixed rule

The tool describes every same-scale position in words before any number, and the words follow one rule. Take the difference between a position and its reference point on the underlying scale: within 0.05 (2.5 display-scale units) the position is "near" the reference; beyond that it is "on the more liberal side" or "on the more conservative side" of the reference. The words give the direction; the chart shows how far, and the exact distance is under "See details". The same rule is used for a senator against the Senate middle, a state's estimate against the national voter estimate, a committee's members against the Senate middle, and the 60-vote point. They are neutral descriptions of relative position, not grades; no senator is ranked.

Units. Both scales are shown on a 0-to-100 display line. A number is a position on that line; it is not a percentage, a vote total, an approval rating or a grade. Differences along one line are given in "display-scale units" (underlying difference × 50). Bill-flow rates are genuine percentages and their differences are "percentage points"; the two are never mixed.

2. How Senate seats represent the country's vote

Every state elects two senators whether it holds 40 million people or 600,000, so a Senate seat represents states equally, not people equally. How much that changes the Senate's political mix can be measured with election results on both sides, so no ideology scale is involved: average each seat's state share of the two-party presidential vote (2016, 2020, 2024, each election weighted equally) and compare it with the national share. Across the 100 seats the average state voted 52.5% Republican; the country voted 49.2%. The mix of states represented by Senate seats is therefore 3.4 percentage points more Republican than the national vote. Taken one election at a time the figure runs from 2.7 to 4.1 points, so it does not depend on which cycle is chosen. This is a fact about which states hold seats, not about any senator's opinions, and the tool describes it without calling it fair or unfair.

2b. The Senate's voting center, and the country's voter estimate

The middle senator's voting pattern sits at 65.5 on the senators' scale, and the 60th senator from the left — the vote that decides whether most legislation can proceed — at 72.0. The national voter estimate, averaging all states weighted by population, is 48.7 on the voters' scale. The two scales come from different measurement systems and are not directly compared.

The tool also shows all 100 senators as one row of circles, each coloured by which side of the Senate middle the senator's pattern falls on: a senator-to-senator comparison.

Every state elects two senators whether it holds 40 million people or 600,000, so the Senate does not represent people in proportion to where they live. That does not decide any single vote, but it shapes which bills can pass at all.

4. What each committee has sent forward

Almost every bill goes to a committee before the full Senate can vote on it, and most are still sitting there. So instead of asking who sits on a committee, we look at what it has actually done: of the bills sent to it, how many has it sent on to the full Senate so far? A bill that has not been sent on is counted as "not yet", never as dead: this Congress is not over, and some will still move.

Each bill takes the side of the senator who wrote it, using that senator's voting record. A bill by a senator with a liberal record counts as liberal. Then, for every committee, we compare how often each side's bills got through.

The rates are stark on their own. Of 5,347 distinct bills sent to a committee this Congress, 425 have been sent on so far — about 8%. (Some bills go to more than one committee, so the committee-by-committee counts add up to 5,376 referrals.) The Finance Committee has sent on 1 of 876.

The adjustment that matters. Across the whole Senate, bills by conservative senators get through 2.4 percentage points more often than bills by liberal senators. Whatever its causes, that Senate-wide difference is the pattern every committee shares. If we compared with zero instead, nearly every committee would look the same way and the measure would say nothing about any one of them. So each committee is compared with that 2.4-point Senate-wide pattern, not with zero.

Every committee's counts are shown on the tool, but the two sides are only compared where each side has at least 25 bills, because below that a single bill moves the percentage by several points. Thirteen of the committees clear that bar.

What is counted, exactly. Senate bills written by current senators, as recorded in the government's bill-status files downloaded on 23 September 2026. "Sent on" means the committee formally reported the bill to the full Senate. A bill approved at a markup meeting but not yet formally reported does not count yet, and House bills sent to Senate committees are not counted, because their authors have no Senate voting record to place them by. So these counts can run below a committee's own tally of bills it has "advanced"; they can be checked bill by bill on Congress.gov.

Why three measures. Members tell you who sits there; the split on its bills tells you how the Senate divided over what it sent on; the rates tell you what it has and has not sent on so far. Each has a weakness the others do not, so the tool shows all three with the count behind each.

What this cannot tell you

It says nothing about any single vote.
A senator's number summarises their whole record into one figure. Someone can match their state overall while voting against it on the issue you care most about.
Politics is squeezed onto one line.
A single liberal-to-conservative axis describes congressional voting well, because most roll-call votes split along it. It describes voters less well, since people's economic and social views do not line up as neatly.
The peer comparison is about senators, not voters.
It says where a record sits among same-party senators from states with a similar recent presidential vote. It does not say whether a senator agrees with, matches or represents the state's voters, and it does not say what a senator should do. Where there are too few peers, or the answer depends on which nearby states are included, the tool says so instead of concluding.
The voter survey and the voting scores are not compared with each other.
The senator scale is built from votes and the state survey scale from survey answers, each on its own footing, and nobody has tied them together. A direct senator-versus-state alignment measure on those two scales requires a validated statistical bridge between voter and legislator scales. CivicAlign does not currently have that bridge, so it makes no such claim; the state comparison it does make uses election results, on the senators' scale throughout.
The state figures are estimates.
They carry one standard error of about 2 points, which varies a little by state and is drawn on the tool as a dashed band around the state's mark. One standard error is a modest band, covering roughly two thirds of the likely values. It is small next to gaps of 30 or 45 points, but it is not zero. The senator scores have their own error too, which Voteview does not publish, so we cannot carry it through — that is the largest remaining hole in the method.
The survey data lags.
The most recent published wave is 2020, built from surveys run 2017–2021. No newer one exists to use. State opinion shifts, so a fast-changing state is described less well than a stable one.
A bill's side comes from its author, not its content.
We label a bill liberal or conservative by the voting record of the senator who wrote it. That is a reasonable stand-in, but a moderate bill written by a conservative senator counts as conservative. Reading the bills themselves would be more precise, and is part of a separate piece of this project.
A bill can sit in committee for reasons other than politics.
Many bills are duplicates, symbolic, or folded into larger bills rather than passed on their own, and this Congress is not over. The low rates reflect that as much as any gatekeeping. The comparison between the two sides is what carries the finding, not the raw rate.

How to check it

Every figure here can be reproduced with a calculator and a spreadsheet. Download the files in the table above. For the peer comparison, take each senator's nokken_poole_dim1 from the Voteview file and their state's two-party presidential share from the MIT file (Republican votes divided by Republican plus Democratic, averaged over the three elections); for a senator, list the same-party senators from other states within 4 points of that share, and see whether the senator's score falls inside their lowest-to-highest range. For the seat comparison, average the 100 seats' state shares and subtract the national share. For a senator's place in the Senate, subtract the median of the 100 scores. For a state's survey estimate, read its mrp_ideology in the ideology file and subtract the population-weighted national figure. Never subtract the ideology file from the Voteview file; the tool does not.

The code that produces these numbers is checked by a test suite that recomputes every published figure straight from the downloaded files, using nothing from the code it is testing — so a bug in the pipeline cannot hide behind a test that shares it. Every download is stamped with its date and a checksum, because Voteview revises scores as new votes are recorded.

If you want to argue with something here, the useful places are the choices rather than the arithmetic: whether one liberal-to-conservative line is a fair summary of politics, whether 2020 survey data is close enough for a 2026 judgement, and whether a bill's author is a fair guide to which side the bill is on. Those are real questions. The arithmetic is not in doubt.
