Methodology · 119th Congress

How we measure Senate representation

We place every US senator and every state's voters on the same scale, then measure the distance between them. This explains where each number comes from and what it can and cannot tell you.

As of 23 September 2026

The question

Does a senator vote the way their state's voters actually lean?

That sounds simple, and the answer is genuinely useful: a senator who sits far from their own state is being sent to Washington by one set of people and voting like a different set. But answering it properly turns out to be harder than it looks, and most of the work goes into one specific obstacle.

The hard part

A senator is measured by how they vote on bills. Voters are measured by what they tell a survey. Those are two different rulers with two different zero points, so subtracting one from the other is like subtracting Celsius from Fahrenheit: you get a number, and it means nothing.

We use the best available data for each side: Voteview's scores for senators, and academic researchers' survey-based estimates for each state's residents. Both run from liberal to conservative and both fall between −1 and +1. But they were built separately, and nobody has tied the two rulers together exactly. The survey scale is set to its own average and spread; the senator scale comes from votes. So when the tool puts the two on one line and subtracts, the result is a rough comparison, good for direction and rough size, not a precise measurement. Every distance on the tool should be read that way.

Everything sits on one line running from −1.0 (most liberal) to +1.0 (most conservative). Senators and state publics both land somewhere on it. Then subtraction is allowed.

Two details that sound small and are not. We use the per-Congress version of the senator score, because the better-known version is a single number fixed for a senator's whole career and could never show anyone changing. And the government's own file lists 104 senators for 100 seats, because members who left mid-term stay in it beside their replacements, so we join against the current roster to get the real 100.

The three measurements

1. A senator against their own state

The tool reports that distance directly, as "0.607 points further Left than the state average". An earlier version turned it into a percentage; that was dropped because readers took it for a share of votes, which it never was.

A distance smaller than the state estimate's own uncertainty is reported as Too close to tell apart rather than as a gap. That band is one standard error of the state estimate, about ±0.04. It is a modest bar: a one-standard-error band covers roughly two thirds of the likely values, not 95%, and the senator score's own error is not published, so it is not included. "Too close to tell apart" therefore means the survey cannot separate the two positions. It does not mean the state's voters agree with the senator, or with each other: a state's number is an average of many people who disagree.

Perspective: what a distance like that means

A position like +0.3 means nothing on its own, so the tool puts landmarks on the same line: the middle Democrat (−0.378), the middle Republican (+0.564), the Senate's middle, the American public, and eight familiar senators at their real positions. The tool marks no bills on the scale. A floor vote's dividing line says where a coalition split, not what a state's voters wanted, so it cannot stand in for either. The senator's number comes from every roll-call vote they have cast this Congress (Ossoff: 842), compared vote by vote with every other senator; the state's number comes from large surveys asking residents where they stand on many policy questions. The gap says how far apart the two sit overall. It cannot say which issues make up the difference.

2. The Senate against the country

Same idea, one level up. The middle senator sits at +0.310. The American public, averaging all states weighted by population, sits at −0.027. The gap is +0.337.

The tool also shows all 100 senators as one row of circles, each coloured by how far the senator sits from the public: 53 of them sit to the public's right. The 60th senator from the left — the vote that decides whether most legislation can proceed — sits at +0.440.

That gap exists because every state elects two senators whether it holds 40 million people or 600,000, and the smaller states lean Republican. It does not decide any single vote, but it shifts which bills can pass at all.

3. Committees: where the members sit, and what they passed

Each committee is measured two ways, both against the Senate's middle (+0.310) and against the public (−0.027).

Where the members sit. The midpoint of the committee's members on the same scale. A midpoint more than 0.15 from the Senate's middle is flagged as a gatekeeper. This number is soft: on a committee split between two parties the midpoint can fall in the gap between them, and it can move sharply when one member is replaced. The last column says how far.

4. Which bills each committee lets through

Almost every bill goes to a committee before the full Senate can vote on it, and most are still sitting there. So instead of asking who sits on a committee, we look at what it has actually done: of the bills sent to it, how many has it sent on to the full Senate so far? A bill that has not been sent on is counted as "not yet", never as dead: this Congress is not over, and some will still move.

Each bill takes the side of the senator who wrote it, using that senator's voting record. A bill by a senator with a liberal record counts as liberal. Then, for every committee, we compare how often each side's bills got through.

The rates are stark on their own. Of 5,333 distinct bills sent to a committee this Congress, 421 have been sent on so far — about 8%. (Some bills go to more than one committee, so the committee-by-committee counts add up to 5,368 referrals.) The Finance Committee has sent on 1 of 872.

The adjustment that matters. Across the whole Senate, bills by conservative senators get through 2.4 percentage points more often than bills by liberal senators. That happens on almost every committee, because Republicans hold the majority. If we left it in, nearly every committee would look biased the same way and the measure would say nothing. So each committee is judged against that 2.4-point baseline, not against zero.

Committees with fewer than 25 bills from either side are left out, because there a single bill moves the percentage by several points. Thirteen of the committees have enough bills to report.

Why three measures. Members tell you who sits there; the split on its bills tells you how the Senate divided over what it sent on; the rates tell you what it has and has not sent on so far. Each has a weakness the others do not, so the tool shows all three with the count behind each.

What this cannot tell you

It says nothing about any single vote.
A senator's number summarises their whole record into one figure. Someone can match their state overall while voting against it on the issue you care most about.
Politics is squeezed onto one line.
A single liberal-to-conservative axis describes congressional voting well, because most roll-call votes split along it. It describes voters less well, since people's economic and social views do not line up as neatly.
The two numbers come from different rulers.
The senator scale is built from votes and the state scale from survey answers, each on its own footing, and nobody has tied them together exactly. Treat every distance as a rough comparison: the direction and the rough size are informative, the second decimal is not.
The state figures are estimates.
They carry one standard error of about 0.04, which varies a little by state and is drawn on the tool as a dashed band around the state's mark. One standard error is a modest band, covering roughly two thirds of the likely values. It is small next to gaps of 0.6 or 0.9, but it is not zero. The senator scores have their own error too, which Voteview does not publish, so we cannot carry it through — that is the largest remaining hole in the method.
The survey data lags.
The most recent published wave is 2020, built from surveys run 2017–2021. No newer one exists to use. State opinion shifts, so a fast-changing state is described less well than a stable one.
A bill's side comes from its author, not its content.
We label a bill liberal or conservative by the voting record of the senator who wrote it. That is a reasonable stand-in, but a moderate bill written by a conservative senator counts as conservative. Reading the bills themselves would be more precise, and is part of a separate piece of this project.
A bill can sit in committee for reasons other than politics.
Many bills are duplicates, symbolic, or folded into larger bills rather than passed on their own, and this Congress is not over. The low rates reflect that as much as any gatekeeping. The comparison between the two sides is what carries the finding, not the raw rate.

How to check it

Every figure here can be reproduced with a calculator. Download the two files in the table above, find a senator in the Voteview file and read their nokken_poole_dim1, find their state in the ideology file and read its mrp_ideology, and subtract. That is the entire calculation.

The code that produces these numbers is checked by a test suite that recomputes every published figure straight from the downloaded files, using nothing from the code it is testing — so a bug in the pipeline cannot hide behind a test that shares it. Every download is stamped with its date and a checksum, because Voteview revises scores as new votes are recorded.

If you want to argue with something here, the useful places are the choices rather than the arithmetic: whether one liberal-to-conservative line is a fair summary of politics, whether 2020 survey data is close enough for a 2026 judgement, and whether a bill's author is a fair guide to which side the bill is on. Those are real questions. The subtraction is not in doubt.
